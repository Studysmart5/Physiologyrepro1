Overview

Core Idea

Reproduction is the process by which male and female gametes develop, grow, and unite to produce a new, unique combination of genes, which becomes a zygote, embryo, and fetus and is finally born. Part 1 covers gametogenesis, sex determination and sex differentiation (SRY, AMH, testosterone, DHT), the gonadal hormones and their pituitary control, puberty and adrenarche, menopause, and the menstrual cycle: the ovarian cycle, the uterine cycle, normal menstruation, and anovulatory cycles.

Learning Points

  • Reproduction includes development, growth, and union of the male gamete (sperm) and female gamete (ovum), then zygote → embryo → fetus in the maternal uterus, and the birth of the fetus.
  • Gametes are produced by gonads: the testes in the male and the ovaries in the female.
  • Sex is set at fertilization by the sex chromosomes: the Y chromosome leads to testes; its absence leads to ovaries.
  • Gonads arise from the urogenital ridge; the SRY gene triggers testis development in the 7th week.
  • Internal ducts and external genitalia develop according to the presence or absence of fetal testes (testosterone and AMH).
  • Gonads have a dual function: gametogenesis and secretion of sex hormones; both androgens and estrogens are secreted in both sexes.
  • Gonadal function depends on pituitary FSH and LH; sex hormones and inhibin B feed back to inhibit them.
  • Adolescence/puberty activates the quiescent gonads; menopause ends ovarian cycles.
  • The menstrual cycle averages 28 days and has ovarian (follicular, ovulatory, luteal) and uterine (menstrual, proliferative, secretory) components.
  • Under SRY, fetal testes secrete AMH (Müllerian regression) and testosterone (Wolffian development); DHT shapes the male external genitalia. Without them the female pattern develops.

Key Definitions

  • Reproduction: the processes by which the male gamete (sperm) and the female gamete (ovum) develop, grow, and unite to produce a new and unique combination of genes in their offspring; it also includes the process by which a fetus is born.
  • Zygote: the new entity formed by gamete union; it develops into an embryo and then a fetus within the maternal uterus.
  • Gonads: organs that produce gametes (testes, ovaries).
  • Genotype: the complete genetic composition of an individual.
  • Sex determination: the sex of the individual, set by genetic inheritance at the moment of fertilization.
  • Sex differentiation: the multiple processes involved in the development of the reproductive system in the fetus.
  • Sex chromosomes: the two chromosomes that determine sex; the larger is X, the smaller is Y.
  • Urogenital (gonadal) ridge: the embryologic site from which both male and female gonads derive.
  • SRY gene (sex-determining region of the Y chromosome): expressed in urogenital ridge cells at the 7th week; triggers testis development.
  • Anti-müllerian hormone (AMH): protein hormone secreted by fetal testes; formerly called Müllerian-inhibiting substance (MIS).
  • Androgens: steroid sex hormones that are masculinizing. Estrogens: steroid sex hormones that are feminizing.
  • Gametogenesis: production of germ cells.
  • Inhibin B: gonadal polypeptide that inhibits FSH secretion.
  • Gonadotropins: anterior pituitary hormones FSH and LH.
  • Adolescence: the period of final maturation of the reproductive system.
  • Puberty (strict definition): the period when the endocrine and gametogenic functions of the gonads first develop to the point where reproduction is possible.
  • Thelarche: development of breasts. Pubarche: development of axillary and pubic hair. Menarche: first menstrual period.
  • Adrenarche: onset of the increase in adrenal androgen secretion at puberty.
  • Menopause: disappearance of sexual cycles because the ovaries become unresponsive to gonadotropins. Perimenopause: the period that precedes menopause.
  • Menstrual cycle: the cyclic change in the female reproductive system of humans and other primates, regarded teleologically as periodic preparation for fertilization and pregnancy.
  • Menstruation: periodic vaginal bleeding with shedding of the uterine mucosa.
  • Antrum: cavity that forms around the ovum in an enlarging follicle, filled with follicular fluid.
  • Atretic follicles: regressing follicles formed by apoptosis (atresia = involution).
  • Ovulation: rupture of the distended follicle with extrusion of the ovum into the abdominal cavity.
  • Corpus hemorrhagicum: the ruptured follicle promptly filled with blood.
  • Mittelschmerz: fleeting lower abdominal pain from minor bleeding into the abdominal cavity causing peritoneal irritation.
  • Corpus luteum: formed when granulosa and theca cells proliferate and clotted blood is replaced by yellowish, lipid-rich luteal cells.
  • Corpus albicans: scar tissue that replaces a degenerated corpus luteum.
  • Anovulatory cycles: cycles in which ovulation fails to occur.
  • Sertoli cells / Leydig cells: cells of the fetal testes; Sertoli cells give AMH, Leydig cells give testosterone.
  • Dihydrotestosterone (DHT): produced from testosterone in target tissue; drives external male development (penis, scrotum; prostate in the flowchart).

Classification / Types

1. Gametes and gonads

  • Male: sperm, produced by the testes.
  • Female: ovum, produced by the ovaries.

2. Sex chromosome complement

  • Male: one X and one Y.
  • Female: two X.
  • Key genotype difference between the sexes arises from this one chromosome.

3. Genital duct systems (before the fetal gonads are functional)

  • Wolffian ducts and Müllerian ducts, plus a common opening to the outside for the genital ducts and urinary system.
  • Male: Wolffian persist, Müllerian regress. Female: the opposite.

4. Sex hormones

  • Androgens (masculinizing) and estrogens (feminizing); both secreted in both sexes.

5. Puberty-related events

  • Girls: thelarche → pubarche → menarche.
  • Both sexes: adrenarche (rise in adrenal androgens, including DHEA).

6. Phases of the menstrual cycle

  • Ovarian: follicular, ovulatory, luteal.
  • Uterine: menstrual, proliferative (preovulatory/follicular), secretory (luteal).

7. Derivatives of the genital duct systems and external genitalia

  • Male (testosterone): Wolffian ducts → epididymis, vas deferens, ejaculatory duct, seminal vesicles; Müllerian ducts regress (AMH).
  • Male (DHT): penis, scrotum, and prostate (flowchart); urogenital sinus forms the urethra.
  • Female (no AMH, no testosterone): Müllerian ducts → uterus, fallopian (uterine) tubes, inner vagina; Wolffian ducts regress.
  • Female: outer vagina and female external genitalia develop; urogenital sinus forms the urethra and lower vagina.

Important Concepts

Sex determination and differentiation

  • In most mammals, male–female differences depend primarily on a single chromosome (Y) and a single pair of endocrine structures (testes or ovaries).
  • Differentiation of primitive gonads into testes or ovaries in utero is genetically determined in humans; however, formation of male genitalia depends on a functional, secreting testis. Without testicular tissue, development is female.
  • Gonads are undifferentiated until the 6th week. In the genetic male, testes begin in the 7th week (SRY expressed in urogenital ridge cells). Without Y/SRY, testes do not develop and ovaries begin to develop in the same area.
  • The internal duct system and external genitalia can develop into either sexual phenotype. Usually most of the tract develops from only one duct system.
  • External genitalia (both sexes) and the outer part of the vagina do not develop from the duct systems but from other structures at the body surface.
  • The fetal testes secrete testosterone and AMH; which duct system and external genitalia develop depends on their presence or absence.
  • Male sexual behavior and (in some species) the male pattern of gonadotropin secretion are due to male hormones acting on the brain in early development.
  • Male pathway: XY → SRY gene → primordial gonads become fetal testes. Sertoli cells secrete AMH; Leydig cells secrete testosterone. The SRY protein induces expression of the AMH gene, and AMH causes degeneration of the Müllerian ducts.
  • Simultaneously testosterone makes the Wolffian ducts differentiate into the epididymis, vas deferens, ejaculatory duct, and seminal vesicles. Externally and somewhat later, DHT (made from testosterone in target tissue) forms the penis, and nearby tissue fuses to form the scrotum; the flowchart also lists the prostate.
  • Female pathway: XX → no SRY → primordial gonads become fetal ovaries. Absence of AMH lets the Müllerian ducts form the uterus, fallopian tubes, and inner vagina; absence of testosterone causes regression of the Wolffian ducts and development of the outer vagina and female external genitalia.
  • Figure details: at 5–6 weeks the embryo is sexually indifferent (gonadal ridge, Wolffian and Müllerian ducts, cloaca). Male differentiation is shown at 7–8 weeks, female at 8–9 weeks. The figure labels the mesonephric duct as forming the vas deferens; fused Müllerian ducts form the uterus; the urogenital sinus forms the urethra (and the lower vagina in the female).
  • External genitalia arise from common primordia (genital tubercle, urogenital fold, labioscrotal fold at 6 weeks). By 10–12 weeks the figure shows male (glans of penis, urethral groove, scrotum, perineal raphe) or female (glans of clitoris, labia minora, labia majora, urethral and vaginal orifices) development.

Gonadal function and its control

  • After birth gonads remain quiescent until adolescence, then are activated by anterior pituitary gonadotropins; their hormones produce adult male/female features and the onset of the sexual cycle in the female.
  • Both secretory and gametogenic functions depend on FSH and LH. Sex hormones and inhibin B feed back to inhibit gonadotropin secretion.
  • In males gonadotropin secretion is noncyclic; in postpubertal females an orderly sequential secretion is necessary for menstruation, pregnancy, and lactation.
  • Androgens are secreted from the adrenal cortex in both sexes. Ovaries secrete large amounts of estrogens and small amounts of androgens; the pattern is reversed in males.
  • In men gonadal function slowly declines with age but the ability to produce viable gametes persists.

Puberty and adrenarche

  • A burst of testosterone occurs in male fetuses before birth; another burst in the neonatal period has unknown function; thereafter Leydig cells become quiescent.
  • All mammals have a period when gonads of both sexes are quiescent until activated by pituitary gonadotropins for final maturation.
  • Gonad removal from soon after birth to puberty causes only a small increase in gonadotropin secretion, so gonadotropin secretion is not being held in check by gonadal hormones (unlike adulthood).
  • At 7–10 years a slow rise in estrogen and androgen precedes a more rapid rise in the early teens.
  • Initial menstrual periods are generally anovulatory; regular ovulation appears about a year later.
  • DHEA peaks at about age 25 in women, slightly later in men, then declines slowly. The rise appears due to increased activity of 17α-hydroxylase.

Menopause

  • Ovaries become unresponsive to gonadotropins with age; associated with, and probably caused by, a decline in primordial follicles that becomes precipitous at menopause.
  • Consequences: (1) no appreciable progesterone and 17β-estradiol; estrogen forms only in small amounts by aromatization of androstenedione in peripheral tissues; (2) uterus and vagina gradually become atrophic; (3) reduced negative feedback, so FSH rises to high levels and LH is moderately high.
  • Perimenopause can last up to 10 years: FSH rises before LH, due to decreased estrogen, progesterone, and inhibins; menses become irregular.
  • Menopause usually occurs between 45 and 55 years; average onset 52.
  • Hot flushes: warmth spreading from trunk to face; with night sweats. Reported in 75% of menopausal women and may continue intermittently for as long as 40 years. An estrogen-sensitive event in the hypothalamus appears to initiate both LH release and the flush episode.
  • It is unclear whether a "male menopause" (andropause) exists similar to that in women.

Ovarian cycle

  • Follicular phase: from birth many primordial follicles lie under the ovarian capsule, each with an immature ovum. At the start of each cycle several enlarge and an antrum forms. Around the 6th day one grows rapidly and becomes the dominant follicle; the others regress as atretic follicles (atresia involves apoptosis).
  • How the dominant follicle is selected is uncertain; it seems related to its ability to secrete the estrogen inside it, needed for final maturation.
  • Main source of circulating estrogen: granulosa cells; but theca interna cells are necessary because they secrete androgens that granulosa cells aromatize to estrogen.
  • Ovulatory phase: at about day 14 the distended follicle ruptures and the ovum enters the abdominal cavity. It is picked up by the fimbriated ends of the uterine tubes (oviducts), transported to the uterus, and unless fertilization occurs leaves through the vagina.
  • Luteal phase: the ruptured follicle fills with blood (corpus hemorrhagicum); minor bleeding may cause mittelschmerz. Granulosa and theca cells proliferate and clotted blood is replaced by luteal cells, forming the corpus luteum, which secretes estrogen and progesterone.
  • Corpus luteum growth depends on adequate blood supply; VEGF appears essential.
  • If pregnancy occurs the corpus luteum persists and usually there are no periods until after delivery. If not, it begins to degenerate about 4 days before the next menses (day 24) and is replaced by scar tissue: corpus albicans.
  • Ovarian cycle in other mammals is similar, but in many species more than one follicle ovulates and multiple births are the rule. Corpora lutea form in some submammalian species but not others.
  • In humans no new ova are formed after birth.

Uterine cycle and menstruation

  • At the end of menstruation all but the deep endometrial layers have sloughed. A new endometrium regrows under estrogens from the developing follicle, increasing rapidly in thickness from day 5 to day 14. Glands lengthen but do not become convoluted or secrete: the proliferative (preovulatory or follicular) phase.
  • After ovulation, under estrogen and progesterone from the corpus luteum, the endometrium becomes highly vascularized and slightly edematous; glands become coiled and tortuous and secrete a clear fluid: the secretory (luteal) phase.
  • Proliferative phase = restoration of the epithelium after the preceding menstruation. Secretory phase = preparation of the uterus for implantation of the fertilized ovum.
  • Secretory phase length is remarkably constant (about 14 days); variation in cycle length is mostly due to variation in the proliferative phase.
  • If fertilization fails the endometrium is shed and a new cycle starts.
  • Mechanism of menstruation: regression of the corpus luteum withdraws hormonal support; endometrium becomes thinner, adding to coiling of spiral arteries; foci of necrosis coalesce; spasm and degeneration of spiral artery walls cause spotty hemorrhages that become confluent and produce the menstrual flow. The vasospasm is probably produced by locally released prostaglandins, present in large quantities in secretory endometrium and menstrual blood.
  • Normal menstruation: blood is predominantly arterial (only 25% venous); contains tissue debris, prostaglandins, and relatively large amounts of fibrinolysin (lyses clots, so clots appear only if flow is excessive). Flow is affected by endometrial thickness, medication, and diseases affecting clotting.
  • Anovulatory cycles: common during the first 12–18 months after menarche and again before menopause. No corpus luteum forms, progesterone effects are absent, estrogens continue; the proliferative endometrium grows thick enough to break down and slough. Bleeding usually occurs in less than 28 days from the last period; flow is variable (scanty to relatively profuse).

Processes / Stages

Reproduction sequence

Gametes develop, grow, and unite → zygote → embryo → fetus (maternal uterus) → birth.

Gametogenesis

Testis

  1. Primary spermatocyte (46 chromosomes).
  2. First meiotic division with crossing-over and homologous chromosome pairing.
  3. Two secondary spermatocytes (23 chromosomes each).
  4. Second meiotic division.
  5. Four spermatids → sperm cells (23 chromosomes).

Ovary

  1. Primary oocyte (46 chromosomes).
  2. First meiotic division with crossing-over: one secondary oocyte (23) and a first polar body (23).
  3. Second meiotic division, with fertilization by a sperm cell (23): zygote (46) and a second polar body (23).
  4. Polar bodies degenerate.

Gonad and duct development

  1. Primordial gonads are undifferentiated in the urogenital ridge until the 6th week.
  2. 7th week, genetic male: SRY expressed, testes develop. No Y/SRY: ovaries develop.
  3. Undifferentiated tract has Wolffian and Müllerian ducts and a common opening.
  4. Fetal testes secrete testosterone and AMH: Wolffian persist, Müllerian regress (female: the opposite).
  5. Male: testosterone → epididymis, vas deferens, ejaculatory duct, seminal vesicles; later DHT → penis, scrotum (prostate). Female: Müllerian ducts → uterus, tubes, inner vagina; outer vagina and external genitalia develop.

Life stages of gonadal activity

  1. Fetal: testosterone burst in male fetuses.
  2. Neonatal: another burst (unknown function); Leydig cells then quiescent.
  3. Quiescent period in both sexes.
  4. Ages 7–10: slow rise in estrogen and androgen; adrenarche at 8–10 (girls) / 10–12 (boys).
  5. Adolescence/puberty: gonadotropin activation; final maturation.
  6. Adult function; ovarian function regresses after years (menopause); testes decline slowly.

Puberty in girls

Thelarche (breasts) → pubarche (axillary and pubic hair) → menarche (first menses; initial cycles anovulatory) → regular ovulation about a year later.

Menopause progression

Perimenopause (up to 10 years; FSH ↑ then LH ↑; irregular menses) → menopause (ages 45–55, average 52) → atrophy of uterus and vagina, FSH high, LH moderately high.

Menstrual cycle (average 28 days; day 1 = first day of menstruation)

  1. Menstrual phase (about days 1–5): endometrial shedding.
  2. Ovarian follicular / uterine proliferative phase (to about day 14): follicles enlarge, antrum forms, dominant follicle by about day 6; estrogen regrows endometrium (days 5–14).
  3. Ovulation (about day 14): follicle ruptures, ovum extruded.
  4. Ovarian luteal / uterine secretory phase (about 14 days, to about day 28): corpus hemorrhagicum → corpus luteum; estrogen + progesterone; endometrium secretory.
  5. Degeneration of corpus luteum about day 24 → corpus albicans; hormonal support withdrawn → menstruation. If fertilization occurs the corpus luteum persists.

Mechanism of menstrual flow

Corpus luteum regresses → hormonal support withdrawn → endometrium thinner, spiral arteries coil → foci of necrosis coalesce → vasospasm (prostaglandins) and degeneration of arterial walls → spotty hemorrhages become confluent → menstrual flow.

Examples

  • Adrenal androgen: DHEA (peak at about age 25 in women); enzyme 17α-hydroxylase.
  • Menopausal symptoms: hot flushes, night sweats.
  • Diseases with increased risk after menopause: osteoporosis, ischemic heart disease, renal disease.
  • Castrate males: show the female body configuration and fat distribution.
  • Male duct derivatives: epididymis, vas deferens, ejaculatory duct, seminal vesicles. Female duct derivatives: uterus, fallopian tubes, inner vagina.
  • Products of gametogenesis: sperm cells (23 chromosomes) from the testis; in the ovary, a zygote (46) after fertilization plus first and second polar bodies (23) that degenerate.
  • Ovarian structures through the cycle: primordial follicle, antral (mature) follicle, corpus hemorrhagicum, corpus luteum, corpus albicans; atretic follicles regress.
  • Male fetal hormones: testosterone, AMH (formerly MIS), and DHT formed from testosterone in target tissue.

Clinical / Practical Importance

  • Menopause: loss of ovarian function causes hot flushes and night sweats, and raises the risk of osteoporosis, ischemic heart disease, and renal disease. Hot flushes occur in about 75% of women and may last up to 40 years.
  • Menstrual blood loss: more than 80 mL is abnormal; flow is influenced by endometrial thickness, medication, and diseases affecting clotting. Clots appear only if flow is excessive (fibrinolysin).
  • Anovulatory cycles are common 12–18 months after menarche and before menopause, with irregular bleeding.
  • Mittelschmerz: brief lower abdominal pain at ovulation.
  • If pregnancy occurs the corpus luteum persists and menses stop until after delivery.
  • Absence of testicular tissue: formation of male genitalia depends on a functional, secreting testis; without it, development is female.
  • First menstrual periods are generally anovulatory; regular ovulation appears about a year later.
