Overview

Core Idea

Reproduction is the process by which male and female gametes develop, grow, and unite to produce a new, unique combination of genes, which becomes a zygote, embryo, and fetus and is finally born. The lecture follows sex determination and sex differentiation, gonadal hormones, puberty and menopause, then the female reproductive system: the menstrual cycle (ovarian and uterine cycles), ovarian hormones (estrogens, progesterone, relaxin), and the feedback regulation of ovarian function. It ends with pregnancy: fertilization and implantation, the endocrine changes of pregnancy (hCG, hCS), and parturition.

Learning Points

  • Reproduction includes development, growth, and union of the male gamete (sperm) and female gamete (ovum), then zygote → embryo → fetus in the maternal uterus, and the birth of the fetus.
  • Gametes are produced by gonads: the testes in the male and the ovaries in the female.
  • Sex is set at fertilization by the sex chromosomes: the Y chromosome leads to testes; its absence leads to ovaries.
  • Gonads arise from the urogenital ridge; the SRY gene triggers testis development in the 7th week.
  • Internal ducts and external genitalia develop according to the presence or absence of fetal testes (testosterone and AMH).
  • Gonads have a dual function: gametogenesis and secretion of sex hormones; both androgens and estrogens are secreted in both sexes.
  • Gonadal function depends on pituitary FSH and LH; sex hormones and inhibin B feed back to inhibit them.
  • Adolescence/puberty activates the quiescent gonads; menopause ends ovarian cycles.
  • The menstrual cycle averages 28 days and has ovarian (follicular, ovulatory, luteal) and uterine (menstrual, proliferative, secretory) components.
  • Estrogens, progesterone, and relaxin have distinct sources, actions, and clinical relevance.
  • Estrogen exerts negative feedback on LH, but a rising level becomes positive feedback and causes the LH surge and ovulation.
  • Under SRY, fetal testes secrete AMH (Müllerian regression) and testosterone (Wolffian development); DHT shapes the male external genitalia. Without them the female pattern develops.
  • Fertilization occurs in the ampulla of the uterine tube; the blastocyst implants in the endometrium through the syncytiotrophoblast, and a placenta develops.
  • In pregnancy the corpus luteum is maintained by hCG; the placenta takes over estrogen and progesterone production after the 6th week; hCS acts as the maternal growth hormone of pregnancy.
  • Parturition (about 270 days from fertilization) depends on estrogen, fetal CRH, and an oxytocin positive feedback loop that ends with expulsion of the fetus.

Key Definitions

  • Reproduction: the processes by which the male gamete (sperm) and the female gamete (ovum) develop, grow, and unite to produce a new and unique combination of genes in their offspring; it also includes the process by which a fetus is born.
  • Zygote: the new entity formed by gamete union; it develops into an embryo and then a fetus within the maternal uterus.
  • Gonads: organs that produce gametes (testes, ovaries).
  • Genotype: the complete genetic composition of an individual.
  • Sex determination: the sex of the individual, set by genetic inheritance at the moment of fertilization.
  • Sex differentiation: the multiple processes involved in the development of the reproductive system in the fetus.
  • Sex chromosomes: the two chromosomes that determine sex; the larger is X, the smaller is Y.
  • Urogenital (gonadal) ridge: the embryologic site from which both male and female gonads derive.
  • SRY gene (sex-determining region of the Y chromosome): expressed in urogenital ridge cells at the 7th week; triggers testis development.
  • Anti-müllerian hormone (AMH): protein hormone secreted by fetal testes; formerly called Müllerian-inhibiting substance (MIS).
  • Androgens: steroid sex hormones that are masculinizing. Estrogens: steroid sex hormones that are feminizing.
  • Gametogenesis: production of germ cells.
  • Inhibin B: gonadal polypeptide that inhibits FSH secretion.
  • Gonadotropins: anterior pituitary hormones FSH and LH.
  • Adolescence: the period of final maturation of the reproductive system.
  • Puberty (strict definition): the period when the endocrine and gametogenic functions of the gonads first develop to the point where reproduction is possible.
  • Thelarche: development of breasts. Pubarche: development of axillary and pubic hair. Menarche: first menstrual period.
  • Adrenarche: onset of the increase in adrenal androgen secretion at puberty.
  • Menopause: disappearance of sexual cycles because the ovaries become unresponsive to gonadotropins. Perimenopause: the period that precedes menopause.
  • Menstrual cycle: the cyclic change in the female reproductive system of humans and other primates, regarded teleologically as periodic preparation for fertilization and pregnancy.
  • Menstruation: periodic vaginal bleeding with shedding of the uterine mucosa.
  • Antrum: cavity that forms around the ovum in an enlarging follicle, filled with follicular fluid.
  • Atretic follicles: regressing follicles formed by apoptosis (atresia = involution).
  • Ovulation: rupture of the distended follicle with extrusion of the ovum into the abdominal cavity.
  • Corpus hemorrhagicum: the ruptured follicle promptly filled with blood.
  • Mittelschmerz: fleeting lower abdominal pain from minor bleeding into the abdominal cavity causing peritoneal irritation.
  • Corpus luteum: formed when granulosa and theca cells proliferate and clotted blood is replaced by yellowish, lipid-rich luteal cells.
  • Corpus albicans: scar tissue that replaces a degenerated corpus luteum.
  • Anovulatory cycles: cycles in which ovulation fails to occur.
  • Aromatase (CYP19): enzyme required for estrogen biosynthesis.
  • Relaxin: polypeptide hormone produced in the corpus luteum, uterus, placenta, and mammary glands (women) and the prostate gland (men).
  • LH surge: burst of LH secretion that produces ovulation.
  • Sertoli cells / Leydig cells: cells of the fetal testes; Sertoli cells give AMH, Leydig cells give testosterone.
  • Dihydrotestosterone (DHT): produced from testosterone in target tissue; drives external male development (penis, scrotum; prostate in the flowchart).
  • Fertilization: union of the ovum and sperm, usually in the ampulla of the uterine tube.
  • Zona pellucida: membranous structure surrounding the ovum; sperm bind to a receptor in it.
  • Acrosomal reaction: breakdown of the acrosome (lysosome-like organelle on the sperm head) with release of enzymes, including the trypsin-like protease acrosin.
  • Fertilin: protein on the surface of the sperm head that mediates fusion with the ovum membrane.
  • Polyspermy: fertilization of the ovum by more than one sperm; prevented by a drop in ovum membrane potential, then by a structural change in the zona pellucida.
  • Blastocyst: the developing embryo as it moves down the tube into the uterus.
  • Syncytiotrophoblast: outer layer of the blastocyst; a multi-nucleate mass with no discernible cell boundaries. Cytotrophoblast: inner layer made of individual cells.
  • Implantation: the blastocyst burrows into the endometrium after the syncytiotrophoblast erodes it.
  • Decidua: the endometrium of pregnancy.
  • Human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG): placental glycoprotein gonadotropin made by the syncytiotrophoblast.
  • Human chorionic somatomammotropin (hCS): protein hormone of the syncytiotrophoblast that is lactogenic and has a small amount of growth-stimulating activity.
  • Parturition: birth; the process by which the uterus expels the fetus.

Classification / Types

1. Gametes and gonads

  • Male: sperm, produced by the testes.
  • Female: ovum, produced by the ovaries.

2. Sex chromosome complement

  • Male: one X and one Y.
  • Female: two X.
  • Key genotype difference between the sexes arises from this one chromosome.

3. Genital duct systems (before the fetal gonads are functional)

  • Wolffian ducts and Müllerian ducts, plus a common opening to the outside for the genital ducts and urinary system.
  • Male: Wolffian persist, Müllerian regress. Female: the opposite.

4. Sex hormones

  • Androgens (masculinizing) and estrogens (feminizing); both secreted in both sexes.
  • Natural estrogens: 17β-estradiol, estrone, estriol.
  • Other gonadal products: inhibin B (polypeptide), progesterone, relaxin.

5. Sources of ovarian and related hormones

  • Estrogens: granulosa cells of ovarian follicles, corpus luteum, placenta.
  • Progesterone: corpus luteum, placenta, and (small amounts) the follicle; small amounts also enter the circulation from testes and adrenal cortex.
  • Relaxin: corpus luteum, uterus, placenta, mammary glands (women); prostate gland (men).
  • Androgens: gonads and adrenal cortex (both sexes); some converted to estrogens in fat and other extragonadal, extra-adrenal tissues.

6. Puberty-related events

  • Girls: thelarche → pubarche → menarche.
  • Both sexes: adrenarche (rise in adrenal androgens, including DHEA).

7. Phases of the menstrual cycle

  • Ovarian: follicular, ovulatory, luteal.
  • Uterine: menstrual, proliferative (preovulatory/follicular), secretory (luteal).

8. Estrogen effects

  • On the female genitalia, on endocrine organs, on the breasts, and on female secondary sex characteristics.

9. Principal targets of progesterone

  • Uterus, breasts, brain.

10. Derivatives of the genital duct systems and external genitalia

  • Male (testosterone): Wolffian ducts → epididymis, vas deferens, ejaculatory duct, seminal vesicles; Müllerian ducts regress (AMH).
  • Male (DHT): penis, scrotum, and prostate (flowchart); urogenital sinus forms the urethra.
  • Female (no AMH, no testosterone): Müllerian ducts → uterus, fallopian (uterine) tubes, inner vagina; Wolffian ducts regress.
  • Female: outer vagina and female external genitalia develop; urogenital sinus forms the urethra and lower vagina.

11. Steps of fertilization

  • (1) Chemoattraction of sperm to the ovum by substances the ovum produces. (2) Adherence to the zona pellucida. (3) Penetration of the zona pellucida and the acrosome reaction. (4) Adherence of the sperm head to the ovum cell membrane, breakdown of the fusion area, and release of the sperm nucleus into the ovum cytoplasm.

12. Placental hormones of pregnancy

  • hCG (luteotropic), hCS (lactogenic, maternal growth-hormone-like), estrogens and progesterone (placenta after week 6).
  • Corpus luteum of pregnancy: estrogens, progesterone, relaxin.

13. Ways oxytocin increases uterine contractions

  • (1) Acts directly on uterine smooth muscle cells. (2) Stimulates formation of prostaglandins in the decidua.

Important Concepts

Sex determination and differentiation

  • In most mammals, male–female differences depend primarily on a single chromosome (Y) and a single pair of endocrine structures (testes or ovaries).
  • Differentiation of primitive gonads into testes or ovaries in utero is genetically determined in humans; however, formation of male genitalia depends on a functional, secreting testis. Without testicular tissue, development is female.
  • Gonads are undifferentiated until the 6th week. In the genetic male, testes begin in the 7th week (SRY expressed in urogenital ridge cells). Without Y/SRY, testes do not develop and ovaries begin to develop in the same area.
  • The internal duct system and external genitalia can develop into either sexual phenotype. Usually most of the tract develops from only one duct system.
  • External genitalia (both sexes) and the outer part of the vagina do not develop from the duct systems but from other structures at the body surface.
  • The fetal testes secrete testosterone and AMH; which duct system and external genitalia develop depends on their presence or absence.
  • Male sexual behavior and (in some species) the male pattern of gonadotropin secretion are due to male hormones acting on the brain in early development.
  • Male pathway: XY → SRY gene → primordial gonads become fetal testes. Sertoli cells secrete AMH; Leydig cells secrete testosterone. The SRY protein induces expression of the AMH gene, and AMH causes degeneration of the Müllerian ducts.
  • Simultaneously testosterone makes the Wolffian ducts differentiate into the epididymis, vas deferens, ejaculatory duct, and seminal vesicles. Externally and somewhat later, DHT (made from testosterone in target tissue) forms the penis, and nearby tissue fuses to form the scrotum; the flowchart also lists the prostate.
  • Female pathway: XX → no SRY → primordial gonads become fetal ovaries. Absence of AMH lets the Müllerian ducts form the uterus, fallopian tubes, and inner vagina; absence of testosterone causes regression of the Wolffian ducts and development of the outer vagina and female external genitalia.
  • Figure details: at 5–6 weeks the embryo is sexually indifferent (gonadal ridge, Wolffian and Müllerian ducts, cloaca). Male differentiation is shown at 7–8 weeks, female at 8–9 weeks. The figure labels the mesonephric duct as forming the vas deferens; fused Müllerian ducts form the uterus; the urogenital sinus forms the urethra (and the lower vagina in the female).
  • External genitalia arise from common primordia (genital tubercle, urogenital fold, labioscrotal fold at 6 weeks). By 10–12 weeks the figure shows male (glans of penis, urethral groove, scrotum, perineal raphe) or female (glans of clitoris, labia minora, labia majora, urethral and vaginal orifices) development.

Gonadal function and its control

  • After birth gonads remain quiescent until adolescence, then are activated by anterior pituitary gonadotropins; their hormones produce adult male/female features and the onset of the sexual cycle in the female.
  • Both secretory and gametogenic functions depend on FSH and LH. Sex hormones and inhibin B feed back to inhibit gonadotropin secretion.
  • In males gonadotropin secretion is noncyclic; in postpubertal females an orderly sequential secretion is necessary for menstruation, pregnancy, and lactation.
  • Androgens are secreted from the adrenal cortex in both sexes. Ovaries secrete large amounts of estrogens and small amounts of androgens; the pattern is reversed in males.
  • In men gonadal function slowly declines with age but the ability to produce viable gametes persists.

Puberty and adrenarche

  • A burst of testosterone occurs in male fetuses before birth; another burst in the neonatal period has unknown function; thereafter Leydig cells become quiescent.
  • All mammals have a period when gonads of both sexes are quiescent until activated by pituitary gonadotropins for final maturation.
  • Gonad removal from soon after birth to puberty causes only a small increase in gonadotropin secretion, so gonadotropin secretion is not being held in check by gonadal hormones (unlike adulthood).
  • At 7–10 years a slow rise in estrogen and androgen precedes a more rapid rise in the early teens.
  • Initial menstrual periods are generally anovulatory; regular ovulation appears about a year later.
  • DHEA peaks at about age 25 in women, slightly later in men, then declines slowly. The rise appears due to increased activity of 17α-hydroxylase.

Menopause

  • Ovaries become unresponsive to gonadotropins with age; associated with, and probably caused by, a decline in primordial follicles that becomes precipitous at menopause.
  • Consequences: (1) no appreciable progesterone and 17β-estradiol; estrogen forms only in small amounts by aromatization of androstenedione in peripheral tissues; (2) uterus and vagina gradually become atrophic; (3) reduced negative feedback, so FSH rises to high levels and LH is moderately high.
  • Perimenopause can last up to 10 years: FSH rises before LH, due to decreased estrogen, progesterone, and inhibins; menses become irregular.
  • Menopause usually occurs between 45 and 55 years; average onset 52.
  • Hot flushes: warmth spreading from trunk to face; with night sweats. Reported in 75% of menopausal women and may continue intermittently for as long as 40 years. An estrogen-sensitive event in the hypothalamus appears to initiate both LH release and the flush episode.
  • It is unclear whether a "male menopause" (andropause) exists similar to that in women.

Ovarian cycle

  • Follicular phase: from birth many primordial follicles lie under the ovarian capsule, each with an immature ovum. At the start of each cycle several enlarge and an antrum forms. Around the 6th day one grows rapidly and becomes the dominant follicle; the others regress as atretic follicles (atresia involves apoptosis).
  • How the dominant follicle is selected is uncertain; it seems related to its ability to secrete the estrogen inside it, needed for final maturation.
  • Main source of circulating estrogen: granulosa cells; but theca interna cells are necessary because they secrete androgens that granulosa cells aromatize to estrogen.
  • Ovulatory phase: at about day 14 the distended follicle ruptures and the ovum enters the abdominal cavity. It is picked up by the fimbriated ends of the uterine tubes (oviducts), transported to the uterus, and unless fertilization occurs leaves through the vagina.
  • Luteal phase: the ruptured follicle fills with blood (corpus hemorrhagicum); minor bleeding may cause mittelschmerz. Granulosa and theca cells proliferate and clotted blood is replaced by luteal cells, forming the corpus luteum, which secretes estrogen and progesterone.
  • Corpus luteum growth depends on adequate blood supply; VEGF appears essential.
  • If pregnancy occurs the corpus luteum persists and usually there are no periods until after delivery. If not, it begins to degenerate about 4 days before the next menses (day 24) and is replaced by scar tissue: corpus albicans.
  • Ovarian cycle in other mammals is similar, but in many species more than one follicle ovulates and multiple births are the rule. Corpora lutea form in some submammalian species but not others.
  • In humans no new ova are formed after birth.

Uterine cycle and menstruation

  • At the end of menstruation all but the deep endometrial layers have sloughed. A new endometrium regrows under estrogens from the developing follicle, increasing rapidly in thickness from day 5 to day 14. Glands lengthen but do not become convoluted or secrete: the proliferative (preovulatory or follicular) phase.
  • After ovulation, under estrogen and progesterone from the corpus luteum, the endometrium becomes highly vascularized and slightly edematous; glands become coiled and tortuous and secrete a clear fluid: the secretory (luteal) phase.
  • Proliferative phase = restoration of the epithelium after the preceding menstruation. Secretory phase = preparation of the uterus for implantation of the fertilized ovum.
  • Secretory phase length is remarkably constant (about 14 days); variation in cycle length is mostly due to variation in the proliferative phase.
  • If fertilization fails the endometrium is shed and a new cycle starts.
  • Mechanism of menstruation: regression of the corpus luteum withdraws hormonal support; endometrium becomes thinner, adding to coiling of spiral arteries; foci of necrosis coalesce; spasm and degeneration of spiral artery walls cause spotty hemorrhages that become confluent and produce the menstrual flow. The vasospasm is probably produced by locally released prostaglandins, present in large quantities in secretory endometrium and menstrual blood.
  • Normal menstruation: blood is predominantly arterial (only 25% venous); contains tissue debris, prostaglandins, and relatively large amounts of fibrinolysin (lyses clots, so clots appear only if flow is excessive). Flow is affected by endometrial thickness, medication, and diseases affecting clotting.
  • Anovulatory cycles: common during the first 12–18 months after menarche and again before menopause. No corpus luteum forms, progesterone effects are absent, estrogens continue; the proliferative endometrium grows thick enough to break down and slough. Bleeding usually occurs in less than 28 days from the last period; flow is variable (scanty to relatively profuse).

Estrogens

  • Biosynthesis depends on aromatase (CYP19): testosterone → estradiol; androstenedione → estrone (the latter also in fat, liver, muscle, and brain).
  • Theca interna cells have many LH receptors; LH acts via cAMP to increase conversion of cholesterol to androstenedione, which they supply to granulosa cells.
  • Granulosa cells make estradiol when given androgens; in primates it is secreted into the follicular fluid. They have many FSH receptors; FSH acts via cAMP to increase aromatase activity. Mature granulosa cells also acquire LH receptors, and LH stimulates estradiol production.
  • Circulating estradiol: 2% free, 60% bound to albumin, 38% bound to gonadal steroid binding globulin (GBG), the same globulin that binds testosterone.
  • In the liver, estradiol, estrone, and estriol are converted to glucuronide and sulfate conjugates, excreted in urine; appreciable amounts are secreted in bile and reabsorbed (enterohepatic circulation).
  • Estrogen comes from the ovary with two peaks: just before ovulation and during the midluteal phase. After menopause secretion declines to low levels.
  • Effects on female genitalia: facilitate growth of ovarian follicles; increase motility of uterine tubes; increase uterine blood flow; make uterine smooth muscle more active and excitable, with more frequent action potentials; the "estrogen-dominated" uterus is more sensitive to oxytocin.
  • Effects on endocrine organs: decrease FSH secretion; inhibit LH (negative feedback) in some circumstances and increase LH (positive feedback) in others.
  • Effects on breasts: duct growth; largely responsible for breast enlargement at puberty ("growth hormones of the breast"); areolar pigmentation (usually more intense in the first pregnancy than at puberty).
  • Female secondary sex characteristics (due partly to estrogens, the "feminizing hormones", and partly to absence of testicular androgens): enlargement of breasts, uterus, and vagina; narrow shoulders and broad hips, thighs that converge, arms that diverge (wide carrying angle); larynx retains prepubertal proportions so the voice stays high-pitched; less body hair and more scalp hair; flat-topped pubic hair pattern (female escutcheon); female fat distribution in breasts and buttocks.
  • Growth of pubic and axillary hair in both sexes is due primarily to androgens, not estrogens. The female body configuration is also seen in castrate males.

Progesterone

  • An important intermediate in steroid biosynthesis in all tissues that secrete steroid hormones.
  • Circulating progesterone: 2% free, 80% bound to albumin, 18% bound to corticosteroid-binding globulin. Short half-life; converted in the liver to pregnanediol, conjugated to glucuronic acid and excreted in urine.
  • In the follicle, theca cells provide pregnenolone to granulosa cells, which convert it to progesterone. Secretion begins to rise late in the follicular phase; the corpus luteum produces large quantities in the luteal phase.
  • LH stimulates progesterone secretion by the corpus luteum via activation of adenylyl cyclase and a later step dependent on protein synthesis.
  • Actions: responsible for progestational changes in the endometrium and cyclical changes in the cervix and vagina; antiestrogenic effect on myometrial cells (decreases excitability, sensitivity to oxytocin, and spontaneous electrical activity; increases membrane potential); decreases the number of endometrial estrogen receptors and increases conversion of 17β-estradiol to less active estrogens.
  • Breast: stimulates development of lobules and alveoli; induces differentiation of estrogen-prepared ductal tissue; supports secretory function during lactation.
  • Feedback effects are complex, at hypothalamic and pituitary levels. Large doses inhibit LH and potentiate estrogen's inhibitory effect, preventing ovulation.
  • It is thermogenic (probably responsible for the rise in basal body temperature at ovulation), stimulates respiration and alveolar PCO2, and in large doses produces natriuresis, probably by blocking aldosterone action on the kidney. No significant anabolic effect.

Relaxin

  • In pregnancy: relaxes the pubic symphysis and other pelvic joints and softens and dilates the uterine cervix, facilitating delivery. Also inhibits uterine contractions and may play a role in mammary gland development.
  • Nonpregnant women: found in the corpus luteum and in the endometrium during the secretory but not the proliferative phase; function unknown.
  • Men: found in semen; may help maintain sperm motility and aid sperm penetration of the ovum.

Feedback regulation of ovarian function

  • Early follicular phase: inhibin B low, FSH modestly elevated, fostering follicular growth. LH is held in check by negative feedback of rising plasma estrogen.
  • 36–48 h before ovulation: estrogen feedback becomes positive, initiating the LH surge that produces ovulation. Ovulation occurs about 9 h after the LH peak.
  • FSH also peaks despite a small rise in inhibin, probably because of strong stimulation of gonadotropes by GnRH.
  • Luteal phase: LH and FSH are low because of elevated estrogen, progesterone, and inhibin.
  • A moderate, constant circulating estrogen level exerts negative feedback on LH; an elevated level during the cycle exerts positive feedback. How negative feedback switches to positive and back to negative in the luteal phase remains unknown.

Fertilization and implantation

  • Fertilization usually occurs in the ampulla of the uterine tube. Millions of sperm are deposited in the vagina; eventually 50–100 reach the ovum and many contact the zona pellucida.
  • Sperm bind to a receptor in the zona, followed by the acrosomal reaction (release of enzymes including acrosin). Acrosin facilitates but is not required for penetration of the zona.
  • When one sperm reaches the ovum membrane, fusion is mediated by fertilin. The fusion provides the signal that initiates development and reduces the ovum's membrane potential, which prevents polyspermy. A later structural change in the zona pellucida gives longer-term protection.
  • The embryo, now a blastocyst, travels down the tube to the uterus in about 3 days, reaching the 8- or 16-cell stage.
  • On contact with the endometrium the blastocyst has an outer syncytiotrophoblast and an inner cytotrophoblast. The syncytiotrophoblast erodes the endometrium and the blastocyst burrows in (implantation), usually on the dorsal wall of the uterus. A placenta then develops and the trophoblast remains associated with it.

Endocrine changes of pregnancy

  • In all mammals the corpus luteum at fertilization fails to regress and enlarges under placental gonadotropins; in humans this is hCG.
  • The enlarged corpus luteum secretes estrogens, progesterone, and relaxin. Progesterone and relaxin help maintain pregnancy by inhibiting myometrial contractions; progesterone prevents prostaglandin production by the uterus, which stops contractions.
  • After the 6th week the placenta produces enough estrogen and progesterone (from maternal and fetal precursors) to take over the corpus luteum's function.
  • Corpus luteum function begins to decline after 8 weeks but it persists throughout pregnancy. hCG secretion falls after an initial marked rise, while estrogen and progesterone keep rising until just before parturition.

Human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG)

  • A glycoprotein (contains galactose and hexosamine) produced by the syncytiotrophoblast.
  • Like pituitary glycoprotein hormones it has α and β subunits; hCG-α is identical to the α subunit of LH, FSH, and TSH. Molecular weight: hCG-α 18,000, hCG-β 28,000.
  • Primarily luteinizing and luteotropic with little FSH activity.
  • Measured by radioimmunoassay; detectable in blood as early as 6 days after conception. Its presence in urine is the basis of pregnancy tests, sometimes detectable as early as 14 days after conception.

Human chorionic somatomammotropin (hCS)

  • Protein hormone secreted in large amounts by the syncytiotrophoblast; lactogenic with a small growth-stimulating activity.
  • Structure is very similar to human growth hormone; GH, hCS, and prolactin appear to have evolved from a common progenitor hormone.
  • Large quantities in maternal blood, but very little reaches the fetus. Maternal pituitary GH secretion is not increased in pregnancy and may be decreased by hCS.
  • Acts as the "maternal growth hormone of pregnancy": nitrogen, potassium, and calcium retention, lipolysis, and decreased glucose utilization; the last two divert glucose to the fetus.

Parturition

  • Pregnancy averages 270 days from fertilization (284 days from the first day of the menstrual period preceding conception). Irregular uterine contractions increase in frequency in the last month.
  • The cervix is firm until near delivery, then softens and dilates while the body of the uterus contracts and expels the fetus. The mechanisms that start labor remain uncertain.
  • Estrogens (from increased circulating DHEAS) make the uterus more excitable, increase gap junctions between myometrial cells, and increase prostaglandin production, causing contractions.
  • CRH from the fetal hypothalamus rises and is supplemented by placental CRH → more fetal ACTH → more cortisol, which hastens maturation of the respiratory system. In a sense the fetus picks the time to be born by increasing CRH secretion.
  • Oxytocin receptors in the myometrium and decidua increase more than 100-fold in pregnancy and peak in early labor. Estrogens raise receptor number; uterine distension late in pregnancy may also increase their formation.
  • Once labor starts, contractions dilate the cervix, which sends signals in afferent nerves that increase oxytocin secretion, creating a positive feedback loop that ends when the products of conception are expelled.
  • Oxytocin acts directly on uterine smooth muscle and stimulates prostaglandin formation in the decidua; prostaglandins enhance oxytocin-induced contractions. During labor spinal reflexes and voluntary abdominal muscle contractions ("bearing down") also aid delivery.

Corpus luteum: pregnancy vs no pregnancyPregnancy: persists, usually no periods until after delivery. No pregnancy: degenerates about day 24, becomes corpus albicans.
Proliferative vs secretory phaseProliferative (preovulatory/follicular): estrogen from follicle, thickening, glands lengthen, no secretion; restores epithelium. Secretory (luteal): estrogen + progesterone from corpus luteum, vascularized, glands coiled and secreting clear fluid; prepares for implantation; constant about 14 days.
Theca interna vs granulosa cellsTheca interna: LH receptors, makes androstenedione from cholesterol (via cAMP). Granulosa: FSH receptors, aromatase makes estradiol (acquires LH receptors when mature); main source of circulating estrogen.
Estradiol vs progesterone plasma bindingEstradiol: 2% free, 60% albumin, 38% GBG. Progesterone: 2% free, 80% albumin, 18% corticosteroid-binding globulin.
Negative vs positive estrogen feedbackModerate constant level: negative feedback on LH. Elevated level 36–48 h before ovulation: positive feedback, LH surge.
Ovulatory vs anovulatory cycleAnovulatory: no corpus luteum, no progesterone effect; estrogen-driven proliferative endometrium breaks down and sloughs (usually within 28 days).
Normal vs abnormal menstrual blood lossNormal: spotting up to 80 mL (average 30 mL), 3–5 days. More than 80 mL is abnormal.
Human vs other mammalsOther mammals: similar cycle, but often several follicles ovulate and multiple births are the rule.
Estrogen vs androgen roles in hair growthPubic and axillary hair growth in both sexes is mainly androgen-driven, not estrogen.
AMH vs testosterone vs DHTAMH (Sertoli cells): Müllerian regression. Testosterone (Leydig cells): Wolffian ducts become epididymis, vas deferens, ejaculatory duct, seminal vesicles. DHT (from testosterone in target tissue, later): penis, scrotum (and prostate).
Male vs female differentiation pathwayMale: SRY present → testes → AMH + testosterone. Female: no SRY → ovaries; absence of AMH and testosterone → Müllerian derivatives (uterus, tubes, inner vagina), Wolffian regression.
Syncytiotrophoblast vs cytotrophoblastSyncytiotrophoblast: outer, multi-nucleate, no cell boundaries, erodes the endometrium, makes hCG and hCS. Cytotrophoblast: inner layer of individual cells.
hCG vs hCShCG: glycoprotein, α/β subunits, luteinizing and luteotropic, maintains the corpus luteum. hCS: protein like GH, lactogenic, maternal growth hormone of pregnancy; low levels signal placental insufficiency.
Corpus luteum vs placenta in pregnancyCorpus luteum (stimulated by hCG) supplies estrogen, progesterone, relaxin early; the placenta takes over after week 6, and the corpus luteum declines after week 8.
Acrosin vs fertilinAcrosin: protease released in the acrosomal reaction, facilitates but is not required for zona penetration. Fertilin: sperm-head surface protein that mediates fusion with the ovum membrane.
Cervix vs body of the uterus at deliveryCervix is firm until near delivery, then softens and dilates; the uterine body contracts and expels the fetus.
Pregnancy length by reference point270 days from fertilization vs 284 days from the first day of the preceding menstrual period.
Progesterone vs estrogen on the uterus in pregnancy and laborProgesterone and relaxin inhibit contractions (progesterone blocks prostaglandin production). Estrogens increase excitability, gap junctions, prostaglandins, and oxytocin receptors, favoring contractions.

Processes / Stages

Reproduction sequence

Gametes develop, grow, and unite → zygote → embryo → fetus (maternal uterus) → birth.

Gametogenesis

Testis

  1. Primary spermatocyte (46 chromosomes).
  2. First meiotic division with crossing-over and homologous chromosome pairing.
  3. Two secondary spermatocytes (23 chromosomes each).
  4. Second meiotic division.
  5. Four spermatids → sperm cells (23 chromosomes).

Ovary

  1. Primary oocyte (46 chromosomes).
  2. First meiotic division with crossing-over: one secondary oocyte (23) and a first polar body (23).
  3. Second meiotic division, with fertilization by a sperm cell (23): zygote (46) and a second polar body (23).
  4. Polar bodies degenerate.

Gonad and duct development

  1. Primordial gonads are undifferentiated in the urogenital ridge until the 6th week.
  2. 7th week, genetic male: SRY expressed, testes develop. No Y/SRY: ovaries develop.
  3. Undifferentiated tract has Wolffian and Müllerian ducts and a common opening.
  4. Fetal testes secrete testosterone and AMH: Wolffian persist, Müllerian regress (female: the opposite).
  5. Male: testosterone → epididymis, vas deferens, ejaculatory duct, seminal vesicles; later DHT → penis, scrotum (prostate). Female: Müllerian ducts → uterus, tubes, inner vagina; outer vagina and external genitalia develop.

Life stages of gonadal activity

  1. Fetal: testosterone burst in male fetuses.
  2. Neonatal: another burst (unknown function); Leydig cells then quiescent.
  3. Quiescent period in both sexes.
  4. Ages 7–10: slow rise in estrogen and androgen; adrenarche at 8–10 (girls) / 10–12 (boys).
  5. Adolescence/puberty: gonadotropin activation; final maturation.
  6. Adult function; ovarian function regresses after years (menopause); testes decline slowly.

Puberty in girls

Thelarche (breasts) → pubarche (axillary and pubic hair) → menarche (first menses; initial cycles anovulatory) → regular ovulation about a year later.

Menopause progression

Perimenopause (up to 10 years; FSH ↑ then LH ↑; irregular menses) → menopause (ages 45–55, average 52) → atrophy of uterus and vagina, FSH high, LH moderately high.

Menstrual cycle (average 28 days; day 1 = first day of menstruation)

  1. Menstrual phase (about days 1–5): endometrial shedding.
  2. Ovarian follicular / uterine proliferative phase (to about day 14): follicles enlarge, antrum forms, dominant follicle by about day 6; estrogen regrows endometrium (days 5–14).
  3. Ovulation (about day 14): follicle ruptures, ovum extruded.
  4. Ovarian luteal / uterine secretory phase (about 14 days, to about day 28): corpus hemorrhagicum → corpus luteum; estrogen + progesterone; endometrium secretory.
  5. Degeneration of corpus luteum about day 24 → corpus albicans; hormonal support withdrawn → menstruation. If fertilization occurs the corpus luteum persists.

Mechanism of menstrual flow

Corpus luteum regresses → hormonal support withdrawn → endometrium thinner, spiral arteries coil → foci of necrosis coalesce → vasospasm (prostaglandins) and degeneration of arterial walls → spotty hemorrhages become confluent → menstrual flow.

Estrogen synthesis

LH on theca interna (cAMP) → cholesterol → androstenedione → to granulosa cells → FSH (cAMP) raises aromatase → estradiol (testosterone → estradiol; androstenedione → estrone) → liver conjugation → urine; bile → enterohepatic circulation.

Hormonal feedback through the cycle

  1. Early follicular: inhibin B low, FSH modestly elevated → follicular growth; estrogen rises and restrains LH.
  2. 36–48 h before ovulation: positive feedback → LH surge (FSH also peaks).
  3. Ovulation about 9 h after the LH peak.
  4. Luteal: LH and FSH low (high estrogen, progesterone, inhibin).

Fertilization to implantation

  1. Chemoattraction of sperm to the ovum.
  2. Adherence to the zona pellucida (receptor binding).
  3. Acrosomal reaction (acrosin) and penetration of the zona.
  4. Sperm head binds the ovum membrane (fertilin); fusion, release of the sperm nucleus into the cytoplasm.
  5. Membrane potential falls (block to polyspermy), then the zona pellucida changes structurally.
  6. Blastocyst travels down the tube (about 3 days, 8–16 cells).
  7. Contact with the endometrium: syncytiotrophoblast (outer) and cytotrophoblast (inner) → erosion and implantation (dorsal wall) → placenta.

Hormonal course of pregnancy

  1. Fertilization: corpus luteum does not regress and enlarges under hCG.
  2. Corpus luteum secretes estrogens, progesterone, relaxin; contractions inhibited.
  3. After week 6 the placenta takes over estrogen and progesterone production.
  4. After 8 weeks corpus luteum function declines; hCG falls after an early rise.
  5. Estrogen and progesterone rise until just before parturition.

Onset of labor and oxytocin positive feedback

  1. Estrogen (DHEAS-derived) and fetal CRH → ACTH → cortisol (lung maturation) prepare the uterus and fetus.
  2. Oxytocin receptors increase more than 100-fold; prostaglandins rise.
  3. Uterine contractions → dilation of the cervix and distention of the vagina.
  4. Stimuli from cervix and vagina (afferent nerves) → increased oxytocin secretion.
  5. More contractions (directly and via prostaglandins) → loop repeats until expulsion of the products of conception.

Examples

  • Natural estrogens: 17β-estradiol, estrone, estriol.
  • Aromatase conversions: testosterone → estradiol; androstenedione → estrone (also in fat, liver, muscle, brain).
  • Adrenal androgen: DHEA (peak at about age 25 in women); enzyme 17α-hydroxylase.
  • Gonadal polypeptide: inhibin B (inhibits FSH). Polypeptide hormone: relaxin.
  • Progesterone metabolite: pregnanediol (conjugated to glucuronic acid, excreted in urine).
  • Binding proteins: albumin, gonadal steroid binding globulin (GBG), corticosteroid-binding globulin.
  • Menopausal symptoms: hot flushes, night sweats.
  • Diseases with increased risk after menopause: osteoporosis, ischemic heart disease, renal disease.
  • Castrate males: show the female body configuration and fat distribution.
  • Male duct derivatives: epididymis, vas deferens, ejaculatory duct, seminal vesicles. Female duct derivatives: uterus, fallopian tubes, inner vagina.
  • Sperm and zona proteins: acrosin (trypsin-like protease), fertilin.
  • Glycoprotein hormones sharing the α subunit with hCG: LH, FSH, TSH.
  • Hormones related to GH: hCS (and prolactin, from a common progenitor).
  • Pregnancy hormones: hCG, hCS, estrogens, progesterone, relaxin.
  • Mediators of labor: oxytocin, prostaglandins, estrogens (from DHEAS), fetal CRH → ACTH → cortisol.

Clinical / Practical Importance

  • Menopause: loss of ovarian function causes hot flushes and night sweats, and raises the risk of osteoporosis, ischemic heart disease, and renal disease. Hot flushes occur in about 75% of women and may last up to 40 years.
  • Postcoital ("morning-after") contraception: large doses of estrogens for 4–6 days after coitus during the fertile period; pregnancy is probably prevented by interference with implantation rather than changes in gonadotropin secretion.
  • Large doses of progesterone inhibit LH secretion and potentiate estrogen's inhibitory effect, preventing ovulation.
  • Basal body temperature rises at ovulation, probably due to thermogenic progesterone.
  • Menstrual blood loss: more than 80 mL is abnormal; flow is influenced by endometrial thickness, medication, and diseases affecting clotting. Clots appear only if flow is excessive (fibrinolysin).
  • Anovulatory cycles are common 12–18 months after menarche and before menopause, with irregular bleeding.
  • Mittelschmerz: brief lower abdominal pain at ovulation.
  • Relaxin relaxes pelvic joints and softens and dilates the cervix, facilitating delivery; in men it may help sperm motility and penetration.
  • Progesterone large doses cause natriuresis (probably blocking aldosterone); it has no significant anabolic effect.
  • The estrogen-dominated uterus is more sensitive to oxytocin; progesterone reduces this sensitivity.
  • If pregnancy occurs the corpus luteum persists and menses stop until after delivery.
  • Pregnancy tests rely on hCG in urine (as early as 14 days after conception); blood hCG is detectable by radioimmunoassay as early as 6 days.
  • Low hCS levels are a sign of placental insufficiency.
  • hCS diverts glucose to the fetus (decreased maternal glucose utilization, lipolysis) while retaining nitrogen, potassium, and calcium.
  • Cortisol raised by fetal CRH and ACTH hastens maturation of the fetal respiratory system.
  • Oxytocin and prostaglandins drive labor through a positive feedback loop; spinal reflexes and "bearing down" aid delivery.
  • Progesterone and relaxin help maintain pregnancy by inhibiting myometrial contractions.

Exam Focus

  • Definition of reproduction; zygote → embryo → fetus; gonads (testes/ovaries) produce gametes.
  • Sex determination at fertilization: XY male, XX female; Y presence → testes; absence → ovaries.
  • Genotype, sex determination vs sex differentiation.
  • Gonads: urogenital ridge, undifferentiated until the 6th week, testes begin the 7th week, SRY gene.
  • Wolffian vs Müllerian ducts in male and female; testosterone and AMH (formerly MIS); external genitalia do not derive from ducts.
  • Gametogenesis chromosome numbers (46 → 23) in testis and ovary, polar bodies.
  • Gonads' dual function; androgens = masculinizing, estrogens = feminizing; both in both sexes; adrenal androgens; peripheral conversion to estrogen.
  • Inhibin B inhibits FSH; gonadotropins FSH and LH; male noncyclic vs female sequential.
  • Puberty: ages (8–13 girls, 9–14 boys), sequence thelarche → pubarche → menarche, adrenarche ages, DHEA peak at 25, 17α-hydroxylase.
  • Menopause: primordial follicle decline, effects, perimenopause up to 10 years, ages 45–55, average 52, hot flushes (75%, up to 40 years), hypothalamic estrogen-sensitive event.
  • Menstrual cycle: 28 days, day 1 = first day of menses.
  • Ovarian cycle: primordial follicles, antrum, dominant follicle (day 6), atresia (apoptosis), ovulation (day 14), corpus hemorrhagicum, mittelschmerz, corpus luteum (VEGF), corpus albicans (day 24).
  • Granulosa vs theca interna: receptors, androstenedione, aromatase (CYP19).
  • Ova numbers: 7 million → 2 million → under 300,000 → about 500; no new ova after birth.
  • Uterine cycle: proliferative (days 5–14) vs secretory (constant about 14 days); variations due to proliferative phase; mechanism of menstruation and prostaglandins.
  • Normal menstruation: 25% venous, fibrinolysin, 3–5 days (1–8), average 30 mL, over 80 mL abnormal.
  • Anovulatory cycles: timing and endometrial effect.
  • Estrogens: three types, sources, plasma binding (2% / 60% albumin / 38% GBG), secretion rates (36, 380, 250 µg/day; men 50), two peaks, all effects (genitalia, endocrine, breasts, secondary sex characteristics).
  • Progesterone: sources, binding (2% / 80% albumin / 18% CBG), pregnanediol, plasma levels (0.3, 0.9, 18 ng/mL), actions (antiestrogenic, thermogenic, natriuresis, no anabolic effect), LH inhibition at large doses.
  • Relaxin: sources, actions in pregnancy, nonpregnant women, and men.
  • Feedback: early follicular (low inhibin B, FSH modestly raised), estrogen positive feedback 36–48 h before ovulation, LH surge, ovulation about 9 h after the LH peak, luteal suppression; the switching mechanism is unknown.
  • Morning-after contraception: high-dose estrogens 4–6 days, probably blocks implantation.
  • Male differentiation: SRY → AMH (Sertoli) and testosterone (Leydig) → DHT; derivatives of Wolffian and Müllerian ducts in both sexes; external genitalia (penis, scrotum vs outer vagina).
  • Fertilization: site (ampulla), the 4 steps, zona pellucida, acrosomal reaction and acrosin (not required), fertilin, block to polyspermy (membrane potential, then zona change); 50–100 sperm reach the ovum.
  • Blastocyst: about 3 days, 8–16 cells; syncytiotrophoblast vs cytotrophoblast; implantation on the dorsal wall; placenta.
  • Pregnancy endocrinology: hCG maintains the corpus luteum; placenta takes over after week 6; corpus luteum declines after 8 weeks; estrogen and progesterone rise until parturition; progesterone and relaxin inhibit contractions.
  • hCG: glycoprotein, α/β subunits (α same as LH, FSH, TSH), MW 18,000 / 28,000, luteotropic, detected in blood at 6 days and urine at 14 days.
  • hCS: lactogenic, GH-like, "maternal growth hormone of pregnancy", effects on N, K, Ca, lipolysis, glucose; low levels = placental insufficiency.
  • Parturition: 270 vs 284 days; cervix vs uterine body; estrogen from DHEAS (gap junctions, prostaglandins); fetal CRH → ACTH → cortisol → lung maturation; oxytocin receptors rise over 100-fold; positive feedback loop of oxytocin; two ways oxytocin acts (direct, prostaglandins in decidua).
