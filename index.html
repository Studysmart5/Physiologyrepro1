The essentials

Not the lecture — just the rules, definitions, and numbers worth knowing cold.

Reproduction

  • The sperm and the ovum develop, grow, and unite to produce a new, unique combination of genes.
  • Zygote → embryo → fetus, within the maternal uterus.
  • Gametes are made by the gonads: testes (male) and ovaries (female).
  • Reproduction also includes the birth of the fetus.

Sex determination

  • The genotype is the complete genetic composition of an individual.
  • Sex is set at the moment of fertilization by inheritance of the sex chromosomes.
  • X is the larger sex chromosome, Y the smaller.
  • Male = XY, female = XX: the key genotype difference is one chromosome.
  • Y present → testes. Y absent → ovaries.

Differentiation of the gonads

  • Testes and ovaries arise from the same site: the urogenital (gonadal) ridge.
  • Primordial gonads stay undifferentiated until the sixth week.
  • In the genetic male the testes develop in the seventh week, triggered by the SRY gene on the Y chromosome.
  • No Y chromosome, no SRY → ovaries develop in the same area.

Internal & external genitalia

  • Before the gonads function, the fetus has a double duct system: Wolffian and Müllerian ducts, plus a common opening for the genital ducts and urinary system.
  • Male: Wolffian ducts persist, Müllerian ducts regress. Female: the opposite.
  • External genitalia and the outer vagina come from other structures at the body surface, not from the ducts.
  • Fetal testes secrete testosterone and AMH (formerly MIS).
  • No testicular tissue → development is female.

Gonadal hormones

  • Dual function: gametogenesis and secretion of sex hormones.
  • Androgens masculinize; estrogens feminize. Both are secreted in both sexes.
  • Ovaries: large amounts of estrogens, small amounts of androgens; reversed in males.
  • Androgens also come from the adrenal cortex; some convert to estrogens in fat.
  • Inhibin B inhibits FSH secretion.
  • FSH and LH drive the gonads; sex hormones and inhibin B feed back to inhibit them.
  • Males: noncyclic secretion. Postpubertal females: sequential secretion, needed for menstruation, pregnancy, and lactation.

Puberty

  • Testosterone bursts occur before birth and in the neonatal period (unknown function); then the gonads are quiescent until activated by pituitary gonadotropins.
  • Strictly, puberty is when gonadal endocrine and gametogenic functions first reach the point where reproduction is possible.
  • Girls 8–13 yr, boys 9–14 yr.
  • Girls in order: thelarche (breasts) → pubarche (axillary and pubic hair) → menarche (first period).
  • Early periods are generally anovulatory; regular ovulation appears about a year later.
  • Adrenarche (rise in adrenal androgens): girls 8–10 yr, boys 10–12 yr.
  • DHEA peaks at about age 25 in women; the rise appears due to increased 17α-hydroxylase activity.

Menopause

  • Ovaries become unresponsive to gonadotropins as primordial follicles decline.
  • No appreciable progesterone or 17β-estradiol from the ovaries; a little estrogen forms by aromatization of androstenedione in peripheral tissues.
  • Uterus and vagina gradually become atrophic.
  • Less negative feedback → FSH rises to high levels, LH moderately high.
  • Perimenopause: up to 10 yr before menopause; FSH rises before LH and menses become irregular.
  • Occurs between 45–55 yr; average onset 52 yr.
  • Hot flushes in 75% of women (up to 40 yr) and night sweats; higher risk of osteoporosis, ischemic heart disease, and renal disease.

Ageing & sexual behavior

  • Male sexual behavior (and, in some species, the male gonadotropin pattern) is due to male hormones acting on the brain in early development.
  • In males, gonadal function declines slowly, but the ability to produce viable gametes persists.
  • Whether a "male menopause" (andropause) exists is unclear.
