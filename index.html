Overview

Core Idea

Reproduction is the process by which the male gamete (sperm) and the female gamete (ovum) develop, grow, and unite to produce a new and unique combination of genes; the resulting zygote becomes an embryo then a fetus in the maternal uterus and is finally born. This part covers gametogenesis, sex determination (X/Y chromosomes), sex differentiation (SRY, AMH, testosterone, DHT), the dual function of the gonads and their pituitary control, puberty and adrenarche, and menopause.

Learning Points

  • Gametes are produced by the gonads: testes in the male, ovaries in the female. Reproduction also includes the birth of the fetus.
  • In most mammals, male–female differences depend mainly on one chromosome (Y) and one pair of endocrine structures (testes or ovaries).
  • Sex is set at fertilization: XY = male, XX = female. The Y chromosome leads to testes; its absence leads to ovaries.
  • Gonad differentiation is genetically determined, but formation of male genitalia needs a functional, secreting testis; without testicular tissue, development is female.
  • Both sexes start with the same gonadal site (urogenital ridge) and a double duct system (Wolffian + Müllerian); testosterone and AMH decide which persists.
  • Gonads have a dual function: gametogenesis and sex-hormone secretion; both depend on FSH and LH, and sex hormones plus inhibin B feed back to inhibit them.
  • Gonads are quiescent from after birth until adolescence, when pituitary gonadotropins activate them.
  • Adrenarche is a separate rise in adrenal androgens at puberty.
  • Menopause: ovaries become unresponsive to gonadotropins, cycles cease, FSH rises; testes decline slowly and viable gametes persist.

Key Definitions

  • Reproduction: the processes by which the sperm and the ovum develop, grow, and unite to produce a new and unique combination of genes in their offspring; it also includes the process by which a fetus is born.
  • Zygote: the new entity formed by union of the gametes; it develops into an embryo and then a fetus within the maternal uterus.
  • Gonads: testes (male) and ovaries (female); they produce the gametes.
  • Gametogenesis: production of germ cells.
  • Genotype: the complete genetic composition of an individual.
  • Sex determination: the sex of the individual, set by genetic inheritance of the sex chromosomes at the moment of fertilization.
  • Sex chromosomes: the larger is X, the smaller is Y.
  • Sex differentiation: the multiple processes involved in the development of the reproductive system in the fetus.
  • Urogenital (gonadal) ridge: the embryologic site from which both male and female gonads derive.
  • SRY gene (sex-determining region of the Y chromosome): expressed in urogenital ridge cells at the 7th week; triggers testis development.
  • Wolffian ducts / Müllerian ducts: the two genital duct systems of the undifferentiated tract.
  • Anti-müllerian hormone (AMH): protein hormone of the fetal testes, formerly called Müllerian-inhibiting substance (MIS).
  • Dihydrotestosterone (DHT): produced from testosterone in target tissue; the main driver of external male genitalia.
  • Sertoli cells / Leydig cells: fetal testis cells; Sertoli cells secrete AMH, Leydig cells secrete testosterone.
  • Androgens: steroid sex hormones that are masculinizing. Estrogens: steroid sex hormones that are feminizing.
  • Inhibin B: gonadal polypeptide that inhibits FSH secretion.
  • Gonadotropins: the anterior pituitary hormones FSH and LH.
  • Adolescence: the period of final maturation of the reproductive system.
  • Puberty (strict definition): the period when the endocrine and gametogenic functions of the gonads have first developed to the point where reproduction is possible.
  • Thelarche: development of breasts. Pubarche: development of axillary and pubic hair. Menarche: the first menstrual period.
  • Adrenarche: the onset of the increase in secretion of adrenal androgens at puberty.
  • Menopause: disappearance of sexual cycles because the ovaries become unresponsive to gonadotropins.
  • Perimenopause: the period preceding menopause (up to 10 years).
  • Hot flushes (hot flashes): sensations of warmth spreading from the trunk to the face.
  • Andropause: the proposed “male menopause”; evidence for it is unclear.

Classification / Types

1. Gametes and gonads

  • Male: sperm, produced by the testes.
  • Female: ovum, produced by the ovaries.

2. Sex chromosome complement

  • Male: one X and one Y. Female: two X.
  • The key genotype difference between the sexes arises from this one chromosome.

3. Genital duct systems (before the fetal gonads are functional)

  • Wolffian ducts and Müllerian ducts, plus a common opening to the outside for the genital and urinary systems.
  • Male: Wolffian persist, Müllerian regress. Female: the opposite.

4. Derivatives of the ducts and external genitalia

  • Male (testosterone): Wolffian ducts → epididymis, vas deferens, ejaculatory duct, seminal vesicles. Müllerian ducts regress (AMH).
  • Male (DHT): penis, scrotum (and prostate in the flowchart); the urogenital sinus forms the urethra.
  • Female (no AMH, no testosterone): Müllerian ducts → uterus, fallopian (uterine) tubes, inner vagina. Wolffian ducts regress.
  • Female: outer vagina and female external genitalia develop; the urogenital sinus forms the urethra and lower vagina.

5. Sex hormones and gonadal secretions

  • Androgens (masculinizing) and estrogens (feminizing), both normally secreted in both sexes.
  • Inhibin B (polypeptide) in both sexes.
  • Ovaries: large amounts of estrogens, small amounts of androgens; the pattern is reversed in males.

6. Pituitary gonadotropins

  • FSH and LH.

7. Puberty-related events

  • Girls: thelarche → pubarche → menarche.
  • Both sexes: adrenarche (rise in adrenal androgens, including DHEA).

8. Stages of plasma testosterone in males (figure)

  • Fetal → Neonatal → Prepubertal → Pubertal → Adult → Senescence.

Important Concepts

Sex determination and differentiation

  • Differentiation of the primitive gonads into testes or ovaries in utero is genetically determined in humans; formation of male genitalia depends on a functional, secreting testis. In the absence of testicular tissue, development is female.
  • Males have one X and one Y, females two X. The presence of the Y chromosome leads to testes; its absence leads to ovaries.
  • The male and female gonads derive from the urogenital ridge and are undifferentiated until the 6th week of uterine life.
  • In the genetic male, testes begin in the 7th week when SRY is expressed in the urogenital ridge cells. Without Y/SRY, testes do not develop and ovaries begin to develop in the same area.
  • The internal duct system and external genitalia can develop into either sexual phenotype. Usually most of the tract develops from only one duct system.
  • The external genitalia and the outer part of the vagina do not develop from the duct systems but from other structures at the body surface.
  • Which ducts and external genitalia develop depends on the presence or absence of fetal testes, which secrete testosterone and AMH.
  • Male sexual behavior and, in some species, the male pattern of gonadotropin secretion are due to male hormones acting on the brain in early development.

Male and female pathways

  • Male: XY → SRY → primordial gonads become fetal testes. Sertoli cells secrete AMH; Leydig cells secrete testosterone (→ DHT).
  • SRY protein induces expression of the AMH gene; AMH causes degeneration of the Müllerian ducts. Simultaneously, testosterone differentiates the Wolffian ducts into epididymis, vas deferens, ejaculatory duct, and seminal vesicles.
  • Externally and somewhat later, DHT (made from testosterone in target tissue) forms the penis, and nearby tissue fuses to form the scrotum; the flowchart also lists the prostate.
  • Female: XX → no SRY → primordial gonads become fetal ovaries. Absence of AMH: Müllerian ducts form uterus, fallopian tubes, inner vagina. Absence of testosterone: Wolffian ducts regress; outer vagina and female external genitalia develop.

Gonadal function and its control

  • After birth the gonads remain quiescent until adolescence, when they are activated by anterior pituitary gonadotropins. Their hormones then produce adult male or female features and the onset of the sexual cycle in the female.
  • In human females, ovarian function regresses after a number of years and sexual cycles cease (menopause). In males, gonadal function declines slowly with age but the ability to produce viable gametes persists.
  • Gonads have a dual function: production of germ cells (gametogenesis) and secretion of sex hormones.
  • Both androgens and estrogens are secreted in both sexes. Androgens are also secreted by the adrenal cortex in both sexes, and some androgens are converted to estrogens in fat and other extragonadal, extra-adrenal tissues.
  • Both the secretory and gametogenic functions depend on FSH and LH. Sex hormones and inhibin B feed back to inhibit gonadotropin secretion.
  • In males, gonadotropin secretion is noncyclic; in postpubertal females an orderly, sequential secretion is necessary for menstruation, pregnancy, and lactation.
  • Feedback loop (figures): GnRH (hypothalamus) → LH and FSH (anterior pituitary) → gonad. Female: theca interna and granulosa cells; feedback by estrogen and inhibin B (androgens act within the ovary). Male: Leydig and Sertoli cells; feedback by testosterone and inhibin B.

Puberty and adrenarche

  • A burst of testosterone occurs in male fetuses before birth; another burst in the neonatal period has unknown function; thereafter the Leydig cells become quiescent.
  • In all mammals there follows a period in which the gonads of both sexes are quiescent until activated by pituitary gonadotropins to bring about final maturation of the reproductive system.
  • From soon after birth to puberty, gonad removal causes only a small increase in gonadotropins, so gonadotropin secretion is not being held in check by gonadal hormones (unlike adulthood).
  • At 7–10 years, a slow increase in estrogen and androgen secretion precedes a more rapid rise in the early teens.
  • Initial menstrual periods are generally anovulatory; regular ovulation appears about a year later.
  • Adrenarche: increase in adrenal androgens at puberty. DHEA values peak at about age 25 in women and slightly later in men, then decline slowly to low values in old age. The rise appears due to increased activity of 17α-hydroxylase.

Menopause

  • Ovaries become unresponsive to gonadotropins with age. This is associated with, and probably caused by, a decline in the number of primordial follicles, which becomes precipitous at menopause.
  • Consequences: (1) ovaries no longer secrete appreciable progesterone and 17β-estradiol; estrogen is formed only in small amounts by aromatization of androstenedione in peripheral tissues; (2) uterus and vagina gradually become atrophic; (3) negative feedback of estrogen and progesterone is reduced, so FSH rises to high levels and LH is moderately high.
  • Perimenopause precedes menopause and can last up to 10 years: FSH rises before LH because estrogen, progesterone, and inhibins fall; menses become irregular.
  • Menopause usually occurs between 45 and 55 years; the average age at onset is 52.
  • Loss of ovarian function causes hot flushes and night sweats and increases the risk of osteoporosis, ischemic heart disease, and renal disease.
  • Hot flushes occur in 75% of menopausal women and may continue intermittently for as long as 40 years. An estrogen-sensitive event in the hypothalamus appears to initiate both the release of LH and the episode of flushing.
  • Testis function declines slowly with age; it is unclear whether a “male menopause” (andropause) exists.

Processes / Stages

Reproduction sequence

Gametes develop, grow, and unite → zygote → embryo → fetus (maternal uterus) → birth.

Gametogenesis

Testis

  1. Primary spermatocyte (46 chromosomes).
  2. First meiotic division with crossing-over and homologous chromosome pairing.
  3. Two secondary spermatocytes (23 chromosomes each).
  4. Second meiotic division.
  5. Four spermatids → sperm cells (23 chromosomes).

Ovary

  1. Primary oocyte (46 chromosomes).
  2. First meiotic division with crossing-over: one secondary oocyte (23) and a first polar body (23).
  3. Second meiotic division, with fertilization by a sperm cell (23): zygote (46) and a second polar body (23).
  4. Polar bodies degenerate.

Sex determination at fertilization

  1. Ova (44 + XX) give mature ova carrying 22X, with the first and second polar bodies (22X).
  2. Primary spermatocyte (44 + XY) gives mature sperms carrying 22Y or 22X.
  3. Fertilization: 22X + 22Y → 44XY (male zygote); 22X + 22X → 44XX (female zygote).

Gonad and duct development

  1. Primordial gonads are undifferentiated in the urogenital ridge until the 6th week.
  2. 7th week, genetic male: SRY expressed, testes develop. No Y/SRY: ovaries develop.
  3. Undifferentiated tract: Wolffian and Müllerian ducts and a common opening.
  4. Fetal testes secrete testosterone and AMH: Wolffian persist, Müllerian regress (female: the opposite).
  5. Male: testosterone → epididymis, vas deferens, ejaculatory duct, seminal vesicles; later DHT → penis, scrotum (prostate). Female: Müllerian ducts → uterus, tubes, inner vagina; outer vagina and external genitalia develop.

Life stages of gonadal activity (male testosterone figure)

  1. Fetal: testosterone burst in male fetuses.
  2. Neonatal: another burst (unknown function); Leydig cells then quiescent.
  3. Prepubertal: quiescent period in both sexes; ages 7–10 slow rise in estrogen and androgen; adrenarche at 8–10 (girls) / 10–12 (boys).
  4. Adolescence/puberty: gonadotropin activation; final maturation.
  5. Adult function; then senescence: ovarian function regresses (menopause), testes decline slowly.

Puberty in girls

Thelarche (breasts) → pubarche (axillary and pubic hair) → menarche (first menses; initial cycles anovulatory) → regular ovulation about a year later.

Menopause progression

Primordial follicle decline → perimenopause (up to 10 years; FSH ↑ then LH ↑; irregular menses) → menopause (ages 45–55, average 52) → no appreciable progesterone/17β-estradiol, atrophy of uterus and vagina, FSH high, LH moderately high.

Examples

  • Gonads: testes (male) and ovaries (female).
  • Male duct derivatives: epididymis, vas deferens, ejaculatory duct, seminal vesicles. Female duct derivatives: uterus, fallopian tubes, inner vagina.
  • Male external derivatives (DHT): penis, scrotum (prostate in the flowchart).
  • Fetal male hormones: testosterone, AMH (formerly MIS), and DHT formed from testosterone in target tissue.
  • Products of gametogenesis: sperm cells (23 chromosomes) from the testis; in the ovary, a zygote (46) after fertilization plus first and second polar bodies (23) that degenerate.
  • Adrenal androgen: DHEA (peak at about age 25 in women); enzyme 17α-hydroxylase.
  • Menopausal symptoms: hot flushes, night sweats.
  • Diseases with increased risk after menopause: osteoporosis, ischemic heart disease, renal disease.

Clinical / Practical Importance

  • Absence of testicular tissue: formation of male genitalia depends on a functional, secreting testis; without it, development is female.
  • Menopause: loss of ovarian function causes hot flushes and night sweats, and raises the risk of osteoporosis, ischemic heart disease, and renal disease.
  • Hot flushes occur in about 75% of menopausal women and may last up to 40 years; an estrogen-sensitive hypothalamic event initiates both LH release and flushing.
  • Perimenopause: FSH rises first, with irregular menses (up to 10 years).
  • First menstrual periods are generally anovulatory; regular ovulation appears about a year later.
  • Male ageing: testicular function declines slowly, viable gametes persist, and a “male menopause” (andropause) is not clearly established.
